Artificial Lift Methods Overview

Video lesson: Artificial Lift Methods Overview

Why a Well Needs Artificial Lift

A well flows by itself while the reservoir pressure is high enough to push the fluid to the surface. Over time the pressure falls, or the fluid gets heavier with water.

  • Natural flow stops when the reservoir pressure can no longer lift the fluid column to the surface.
  • Artificial lift adds energy to the well: it either makes the fluid column lighter, or pushes or pumps the fluid up.
  • There are two groups: gas lift (lighten the column with gas) and pumping methods (add pressure with a pump).
  • No method is best everywhere. The choice depends on the depth, rate, fluid, gas, sand, temperature, well shape, power and cost.
  • The aim is to lower the flowing bottom hole pressure so that the reservoir gives more fluid.
Lift does not make the reservoir bigger. It lowers the pressure at the bottom of the well so the reservoir can flow more.

Gas Lift

Gas lift injects gas into the well to make the fluid column lighter.

  • Gas is injected down the annulus, the space between the tubing and the casing, and enters the tubing through gas lift valves.
  • The gas mixes with the liquid, the mixture is lighter, and the reservoir pressure can lift it.
  • It has no moving parts in the well except the valves, and it handles sand and high gas well.
  • It needs a gas source and a compressor and a supply line.
  • On a well test, the injected gas is part of the gas measured at the surface. It must be known and removed from the well gas rate.
In a gas lift well test, always ask how much lift gas is being injected, and record it.

Pumping Methods

Four common pumps add pressure to the fluid inside the well.

  • Beam pump (sucker rod): a surface pump jack moves a string of rods up and down. A plunger in a barrel, with a standing and a travelling valve, lifts the fluid on every stroke. Common for shallow and medium wells with lower rates.
  • ESP (electrical submersible pump): a multi-stage centrifugal pump with an electric motor, run on a cable from the surface. Good for high rates, and sensitive to gas and sand.
  • PCP (progressing cavity pump): a screw-shaped rotor turns inside a rubber-lined stator. Good for heavy oil and some sand, and sensitive to heat and some chemicals.
  • Hydraulic pump: a pump driven by high pressure power fluid sent from the surface (piston or jet type). It can work at depth and with some gas, and needs a power fluid system.
Match the pump to the problem: gas and sand need one design, heavy oil another, and high rate another.

Choosing a Method and Well Testing a Lifted Well

Each method has strengths and limits, and each changes how a well test is run.

  • Compare the rate, depth, gas, sand, temperature, power available, fluid viscosity, and cost.
  • Plan the test with the well engineer, who knows the lift equipment and its limits.
  • Keep the lift running stable during the test: the speed of a pump, the stroke rate or the lift gas rate. Record it.
  • Gas lift gas is measured and subtracted. A pump speed change changes the rate, so record the time of every change.
  • Know the shutdown and restart rules of the lift equipment. Not every pump can be stopped or restarted at will.
  • Follow your company and site procedures for electric, rotating and pressure hazards.
A steady lift setting gives a steady test. Changes must be written with the time.
