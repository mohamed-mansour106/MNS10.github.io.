How to Operate Transfer Centrifugal Pump

1- General about Transfer Centrifugal Pump

  • What is a Transfer Centrifugal Pump?

    A Transfer Centrifugal Pump is used to move liquids such as crude oil, produced water, diesel, or other fluids from one location to another within an oilfield facility.

    Heavy Duty Casing and Impeller

    Transfer Centrifugal Pumps are equipped with heavy-duty components designed to withstand harsh oilfield operating conditions.

    1. Casing (Pump Body) – The outer housing that contains and directs the fluid through the pump.
    2. Impeller – The rotating component that transfers energy to the liquid and creates flow.

    These components are designed for continuous service, durability, and resistance to wear in demanding field operations.

    Low Suction Pressure Head

    Transfer pumps normally operate with relatively low suction pressure. They commonly draw liquids from storage tanks, atmospheric vessels, or low-pressure process equipment.

    The impeller then increases the liquid's energy and pushes it through the discharge system.

    Moderate Discharge Pressure

    Transfer Centrifugal Pumps are intended for liquid transfer rather than high-pressure injection applications.

    Typical discharge pressure ranges from:

    • 50 – 150 psig
    • Approximately 3.5 – 10 bar

    This pressure range is sufficient for transferring fluids through pipelines, tanks, and process equipment.

    Single-Stage and Two-Stage Pumps

    Transfer Centrifugal Pumps may be designed as either:

    1. Single-Stage Pump
      • Contains one impeller.
      • Simpler design.
      • Suitable for lower discharge pressure requirements.
    2. Two-Stage Pump
      • Contains two impellers arranged in series.
      • Provides higher discharge pressure.
      • Used for longer transfer distances or higher system resistance.
    Pump Capacity

    The liquid flow rate depends on several factors:

    • Suction line size.
    • Discharge line size.
    • Impeller diameter and design.
    • Pump rotational speed.

    Larger piping and impellers generally provide higher flow capacity.

    Pump Drive Types

    Transfer Centrifugal Pumps can be powered by:

    1. Electric Motor Drive
      • Quiet operation.
      • High efficiency.
      • Low maintenance requirements.
    2. Engine Drive (Diesel/Gas)
      • Suitable for remote locations.
      • Does not require electrical power.
      • Commonly used in temporary well testing operations.
    Explosion-Proof and Zone II Requirements

    Oil and gas facilities often contain flammable gases and vapors. Therefore, transfer pumps and associated equipment must comply with hazardous-area requirements.

    • Explosion-Proof: Designed to prevent ignition of the surrounding atmosphere.
    • Zone II: Suitable for areas where explosive gases are not normally present but may occasionally occur.
    Sour Service Requirements

    When Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S) or Carbon Dioxide (CO₂) is present in the production stream, the pump must be suitable for Sour Service applications.

    • Corrosion-resistant materials are required.
    • Special seals and elastomers may be necessary.
    • Equipment should comply with NACE sour-service standards.

    Sour-service pumps provide safe and reliable operation in corrosive production environments.

2- Utilization of Transfer Centrifugal Pumps

  • General Function

    Centrifugal Pumps are mainly used to transfer different types of liquids and liquid mixtures containing small amounts of gas.

    Typical fluids handled include:

    • Water
    • Brine
    • Crude Oil
    • Diesel
    • Drilling Mud
    • Chemicals
    • Additives
    1. Loading Gantry Operations

    A Centrifugal Pump can be connected to a Loading Gantry Skid to transfer liquids from storage tanks into road transportation trucks.

    This operation is commonly used for exporting crude oil, produced water, or other liquid products from the site.

    2. Mixing H₂S Scavenger Chemicals with Sour Oil

    The pump can be connected between the inlet and discharge of storage tanks to circulate sour oil and mix it with H₂S scavenger chemicals.

    The purpose is to:

    • Reduce Hydrogen Sulfide (H₂S) concentration.
    • Improve safety during storage and transportation.
    • Lower tank H₂S content to approximately 15–30 ppm.
    3. Acidic Water Treatment

    The pump can circulate acidic water inside storage tanks while mixing treatment chemicals.

    The objective is to:

    • Increase pH value.
    • Reduce water acidity.
    • Improve pH from approximately 3–5 to 7–8.
    4. Booster Pump for High-Pressure Pumps

    A Centrifugal Pump may be installed on the suction side of a High-Pressure (HP) Pump and operate as a Booster Pump.

    Its purpose is to:

    • Provide adequate Net Positive Suction Head (NPSH).
    • Improve suction conditions.
    • Reduce the risk of cavitation.
    • Increase pump reliability and performance.
    5. Surge Tank Unloading

    The pump can be connected to the discharge line of a Surge Tank to transfer liquids to the Flare Burner system.

    This application assists in safe liquid disposal and tank management during well testing operations.

    6. Meter Testing and Calibration

    The pump can be connected to Calibration Surge Tanks or Gauge Tanks to circulate fluids through flow meters during testing and calibration.

    This ensures:

    • Meter functionality verification/testing.
    • Flow measurement accuracy.
    • Proper meter calibration.
    7. Offshore Water Screening and Burner Cooling

    On offshore installations, Centrifugal Pumps are used in water screening systems and flare burner cooling systems.

    Their functions include:

    • Providing filtered seawater for operational systems.
    • Supplying cooling water to flare burners.
    • Protecting equipment from overheating.

3- Work Roles and Responsibilities

  • 5.1 Electrician Responsibilities

    The electrician is responsible for properly connecting the electric transfer centrifugal pump to a suitable power source.

    The power source may be:

    • Portable Generator Distribution Panel.
    • Building Electrical Power Supply.

    The electrician shall ensure that all electrical connections are safe, correctly installed, properly grounded, and suitable for the pump's voltage and power requirements.

    Electrician Key Duties
    • Connect the pump to the designated power source.
    • Verify voltage and frequency requirements.
    • Ensure proper grounding (earthing).
    • Inspect electrical cables and connections.
    • Confirm electrical safety before startup.
    5.2 SWT Supervisor and Team Responsibilities

    The SWT Supervisor and crew members are responsible for rigging up, installing, and operating the centrifugal pump in accordance with the approved P&ID and equipment layout drawings.

    Rig-Up Activities

    Rig-up refers to preparing and installing the pump and associated piping before operation.

    • Positioning the pump correctly.
    • Connecting suction lines.
    • Connecting discharge lines.
    • Installing valves and fittings.
    • Checking all connections before startup.
    Suction and Discharge Connections

    The SWT team shall properly connect temporary process lines to the pump:

    • Suction Line: The line through which fluid enters the pump.
    • Discharge Line: The line through which fluid leaves the pump.

    All connections must follow the approved P&ID and equipment layout.

    Pump Operation Responsibilities

    After installation, the SWT Supervisor and crew are responsible for operating the pump safely and efficiently.

    • Start the pump according to operating procedures.
    • Monitor pressure and flow conditions.
    • Inspect for leaks or abnormal vibrations.
    • Ensure continuous safe operation.
    • Report any equipment malfunction immediately.
    Shutdown and Isolation Responsibilities

    Whenever the pump is not in use, the SWT Supervisor and crew shall:

    • Switch off the pump.
    • Isolate the electrical power source.
    • Secure the equipment against accidental startup.
    • Follow site safety procedures.

    Proper isolation protects personnel, equipment, and the worksite from potential hazards.

4- Definition of Terms

  • 1. MPS Technician

    An authorized MPS-SWT employee who possesses the technical knowledge and skills required to perform Preventive Maintenance (PM) activities on equipment and machinery.

    Typical responsibilities include inspection, servicing, adjustment, troubleshooting, and maintenance of equipment to ensure reliable operation.

    2. Machine Operator

    A qualified MPS-SWT employee who is authorized to operate machinery or equipment in a controlled and safe manner.

    The operator must understand equipment functions, operating limits, safety requirements, and the potential impacts of incorrect operation.

    3. Energy Isolating Device

    A mechanical device that physically prevents the transmission, release, or supply of energy to equipment.

    Energy isolating devices are used to ensure equipment remains safely de-energized during maintenance or repair activities.

    Examples include:

    • Manually operated electrical circuit breaker.
    • ON/OFF switch with a visible position indicator (I/O).

    Note: Green/Red push-button selector switches are not considered energy isolating devices because they do not physically isolate the energy source.

    4. Lock-Out Device

    A device that uses a lock and key to secure an energy isolating device in the safe or OFF position.

    The purpose of a lock-out device is to prevent equipment from being accidentally energized while maintenance or repair work is being performed.

    5. Tag-Out Device

    A prominent warning tag that is securely attached to equipment, machinery, or an energy isolating device.

    The tag identifies the person responsible for the isolation and warns personnel not to operate or remove the isolation without authorization.

    6. Example of an Energy Control Device

    A come-a-long or similar mechanical restraint used to hold a component in a safe position must be tagged and identified as an energy control device.

    It must not be removed except by the person who originally installed it or by an authorized individual following the proper procedure.

    7. Motor

    A motor is an electrically energized device that converts electrical energy into mechanical rotational energy.

    The motor contains a high-speed rotating shaft that drives another mechanical device such as a centrifugal pump.

    8. Centrifugal Pump

    A centrifugal pump is a mechanical device used to transport or move fluids by utilizing kinetic energy generated by a high-speed rotating impeller.

    As the impeller rotates, it increases the velocity of the fluid and converts that velocity into pressure energy, allowing the fluid to be transferred through the system.

5- Checks Prior to Operating a Centrifugal Pump

  • 1. Verify Lock-Out / Tag-Out (LOTO) Status

    Ensure that all Lock-Out / Tag-Out (LOTO) devices have been removed and the pump is authorized for operation.

    Equipment must never be started while LOTO procedures are still active.

    2. Identify Electrical Isolation Devices

    Locate and identify the circuit breakers, magnetic contactors, and control panel associated with the pump.

    Operators should know how to isolate power quickly in case of an emergency.

    3. Check Earthing System

    Verify that the earthing cable and earthing rod are properly connected to the designated grounding points.

    Proper grounding protects personnel and equipment from electrical faults and static electricity hazards.

    4. Inspect Electrical Cable Condition

    The electrician shall inspect the electrical cable for:

    • Continuity.
    • Correct cable length.
    • Proper cable size and rating.
    • Functional operation.
    5. Verify Impeller Rotation Direction

    Ensure that the pump impeller rotates in the correct direction as indicated by the rotation arrow on the pump casing.

    Incorrect rotation may result in poor performance, low flow rate, and potential equipment damage.

    6. Check Mechanical Coupling

    Inspect the coupling between the motor shaft and pump shaft.

    Ensure the coupling is properly aligned, secure, and free from visible damage or excessive wear.

    7. Inspect Mechanical Seal

    Check the mechanical seal condition, seal oil level, and inspect for any signs of leakage around the shaft and pump casing.

    8. Check Pump Pipework and Pressure Gauge

    Verify the continuity and integrity of both suction and discharge pipework connections.

    Ensure that a pressure gauge is installed on the discharge line for monitoring pump performance during operation.

6- Operating a Centrifugal Pump

  • Step 1: Open the Suction Valve

    Open the suction ball valve and ensure the pump is properly filled with liquid.

    Remove any trapped air by checking and venting through the sample point.

    Step 2: Open the Discharge Bypass Line

    Open the discharge bypass line returning to the tank inlet to establish a circulation loop.

    This helps maintain liquid movement and eliminates potential air locks before directing flow to the final destination.

    Step 3: Recheck Mechanical Seal Condition

    Verify the mechanical seal oil level and inspect for leakage between the pump casing and shaft before startup.

    Step 4: Start the Pump

    Switch the pump ON (I) and observe the discharge pressure gauge.

    Expected discharge pressure is typically between 0 and 50 psig during circulation.

    Step 5: Verify Liquid Circulation

    Confirm that the pump is circulating liquid from the tank and returning it back through the bypass line.

    Stable pressure and continuous flow indicate proper operation.

    Step 6: Check for Air Lock

    If the pump is not circulating liquid, an air lock may be present.

    Immediately stop the pump, remove trapped air, and resolve the issue before restarting.

    Step 7: Restart and Monitor Pressure

    Restart the pump and continue monitoring the discharge pressure to ensure stable operation.

    Step 8: Direct Flow to the Required Destination

    Open the discharge ball valve and gradually close the bypass valve.

    The pump may now transfer liquid to:

    • Flare Burner System.
    • Loading Truck.
    • High-Pressure Booster System.
    • Other Process Equipment.
    Step 9: Monitor for Leaks

    Continuously inspect the pump, piping, and surrounding area for any liquid leakage.

    Stop the pump immediately if significant leakage is observed.

    Step 10: Monitor Tank Level

    Observe the tank level through the sight glass and ensure the liquid level decreases as expected during transfer operations.

    Step 11: Stop the Pump

    Stop the pump when the tank level reaches 0.0 barrels (Bbls) or when transfer operations have been completed.

    Follow site procedures for shutdown and electrical isolation if the pump is no longer required.
