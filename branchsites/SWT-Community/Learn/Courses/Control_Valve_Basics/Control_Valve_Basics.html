Control Valve Basics

Video lesson: Control Valve Basics

What a Control Valve Does

A control valve changes the flow in a line to keep a process value at its set point.

  • A controller compares the measured value, for example level or pressure, with the set point.
  • The controller sends a signal to the valve, and the valve opens or closes to correct the difference.
  • This is called a control loop: sensor, controller, valve.
  • Examples: a level control valve on a separator liquid line, and a pressure control valve on the gas line.
  • A control valve is not an isolation valve. It is not made to seal tightly, so use isolation valves for isolation.
Sensor measures, controller decides, valve acts. All three must work for the loop to work.

Main Parts

A control valve has a body with trim, and an actuator on top.

  • The body holds the pressure and connects to the pipe.
  • The trim is the inside: the plug, the seat and the cage. It sets how the flow changes with the opening.
  • The stem links the plug to the actuator.
  • The actuator moves the stem. A common type uses air pressure on a diaphragm against a spring.
  • The positioner makes sure the valve goes to the position the controller asks for.
  • The packing seals around the stem.
Trim parts wear first because the fluid passes them at high speed.

Body Types and Fail Action

Different bodies suit different services.

  • Globe valves give good control and are the most common control valve.
  • Ball and butterfly valves with special trim are used for larger flow and lower pressure drop.
  • Fail action is where the valve goes if the air or signal is lost: fail closed, fail open or fail in last position.
  • Fail action is chosen for safety: for example a fuel gas valve fails closed, and a cooling valve may fail open.
  • Air to open and air to close describe how the air acts. Check the tag and the design before work.
Always know the fail action of a valve before you isolate its air or signal.

Sizing, Problems and Care

A valve that is too big or too small controls badly.

  • The flow coefficient (Cv) shows how much flow a valve passes. It is used to size the valve.
  • An oversized valve works almost closed and hunts. An undersized valve cannot pass the flow.
  • Cavitation and flashing happen when the pressure falls so low that bubbles form. They damage the trim and make noise.
  • Sticking stems, dirty air, worn packing and clogged trim are common faults.
  • Clean air supply, regular stroke tests and records keep the valve healthy.
  • Follow your company and site procedures to isolate and depressurize before working on a valve.
Stroke test: command the valve 0 to 100 percent and watch it follow. It finds many faults early.
