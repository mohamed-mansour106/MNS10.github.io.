How to Estimate of Shrinkage Factor

What is Shrinkage Factor?

When crude oil is produced from the reservoir, it contains a significant amount of dissolved gas. As the fluid flows through the separator, pressure decreases and part of this dissolved gas comes out of solution (gas flashes off).

Because some of the gas leaves the liquid phase, the volume of oil becomes smaller. This reduction in oil volume is known as Oil Shrinkage.

Simple Idea:
Reservoir Oil Volume → Separator Oil Volume → Stock Tank Oil Volume
The oil volume continuously decreases as gas is released.

Why is Shrinkage Important?

During well testing, engineers need to estimate how much stock tank oil will be obtained from the measured separator oil volume.

Since laboratory PVT data is not always available in the field, empirical shrinkage correlations are used to estimate this volume reduction.

What Does the Shrinkage Factor Represent?

The Shrinkage Factor represents the relationship between:

  • Separator Oil Volume
  • Stock Tank Oil Volume

It indicates how much liquid volume remains after dissolved gas has been removed.

Shrinkage Factor = Stock Tank Oil Volume ÷ Separator Oil Volume

Example

If 100 barrels of separator oil produce only 84 barrels of stock tank oil:

Shrinkage Factor = 84 ÷ 100 = 0.84

This means the oil experienced a volume reduction of:

Oil Shrinkage = (1 − 0.84) × 100 = 16%

Parameters Affecting Shrinkage Factor

  • Separator Pressure: Higher pressure keeps more gas dissolved.
  • Separator Temperature: Influences gas liberation from oil.
  • Gas Specific Gravity: Heavier gases affect shrinkage behavior.
  • Oil API Gravity: Light oils generally experience greater shrinkage.
  • Observed Temperature: Used to correct gas gravity calculations.
Field Application:
Shrinkage Factor is widely used during well testing to estimate stock tank oil production when laboratory PVT analysis is unavailable.

Why Does Oil Shrinkage Happen?

Crude oil inside the reservoir exists under very high pressure. Under these conditions, a large amount of natural gas remains dissolved in the oil, similar to carbon dioxide dissolved in a sealed soft drink bottle.

Think of a soda bottle:
When the bottle is closed, the gas stays dissolved in the liquid. Once opened, pressure drops and gas bubbles escape.

Shrinkage Process

Reservoir Oil ↓
High Pressure → Gas Dissolved in Oil ↓
Separator Pressure Drops ↓
Gas Liberated (Flashes Off) ↓
Oil Volume Decreases ↓
Shrinkage Occurs

As the produced fluid enters the separator, pressure decreases significantly compared with reservoir pressure. This pressure reduction causes part of the dissolved gas to leave the oil and become free gas.

Since some of the volume previously occupied by dissolved gas is removed from the liquid, the remaining oil occupies less space. Therefore, the oil volume decreases.

Example

Suppose 100 barrels of separator oil contain dissolved gas. After stabilization in the stock tank, some gas escapes and only 84 barrels of oil remain.

Separator Oil = 100 bbl ↓ Gas Released = 16% ↓ Stock Tank Oil = 84 bbl
Key Engineering Concept

The more dissolved gas the oil contains, the greater the shrinkage will be when pressure decreases.

Light oils (high API gravity) generally contain more dissolved gas and therefore experience greater shrinkage than heavy oils.

Required Inputs for Shrinkage Factor Calculation

To estimate the oil shrinkage factor, several field measurements are required. Each parameter affects how much gas leaves the oil when pressure and temperature change.

1. Separator Pressure (Psep)

Separator Pressure is the operating pressure inside the separator where oil and gas are separated.

Unit = psig

Higher separator pressure keeps more gas dissolved in the oil. Therefore, less gas is released and shrinkage becomes smaller.

High Psep → Less Gas Liberated → Less Shrinkage

2. Separator Temperature (Tsep)

Separator Temperature is the temperature of the fluid inside the separator.

Unit = °F

As temperature increases, gas becomes less soluble in oil and more gas is released. This results in greater oil shrinkage.

High Tsep → More Gas Released → More Shrinkage

3. Gas Specific Gravity (γg)

Gas Specific Gravity compares the density of produced gas with the density of air.

γg = Gas Density / Air Density

It indicates whether the gas is light or heavy and affects the shrinkage correlation.

γg = 0.65 → Light Gas γg = 0.90 → Heavy Gas

4. Oil API Gravity (API)

API Gravity measures how light or heavy the crude oil is compared to water.

API = Lightness of Crude Oil

Light oils usually contain more dissolved gas than heavy oils. Therefore, they generally experience greater shrinkage.

20 API → Heavy Oil 45 API → Light Oil
Higher API → More Dissolved Gas → Higher Shrinkage

5. Observed Temperature (Tobs)

Observed Temperature is the temperature at which the gas gravity was measured.

Unit = °F

Since gas density changes with temperature, the observed gas gravity must be corrected to standard conditions before calculating the shrinkage factor.

Tobs is used to calculate the Adjusted Gas Gravity (γg adj).
Important Note

Accurate field measurements of pressure, temperature, gas gravity, and API gravity are essential because small errors can significantly affect the final shrinkage factor.

Calculation Logic – Step by Step

The Shrinkage Factor calculation is performed through a sequence of empirical correlations. Each step uses the result of the previous step until the final shrinkage factor is obtained.

Step 1 – Gas Gravity Correction

Gas S.G. ($$yg_{adj}) = \gamma_g + [0.00069-(0.0003725\gamma_g)](T_{obs}-60)$$

Gas specific gravity is normally measured at a temperature different from the standard reference temperature (60°F). Therefore, the first step is to correct the observed gas gravity.

Purpose: Adjust gas gravity to standard conditions before using it in the shrinkage correlation.

Output: Adjusted Gas Gravity (ygadj)

Step 2 – Calculate F1

$$F1=\left(\frac{1.797}{yg_{adj}}-1.838\right)P_{sep}$$

This parameter combines the effects of separator pressure and corrected gas gravity.

Purpose: Determine how separator operating conditions influence gas liberation.

Higher separator pressure generally reduces gas liberation and therefore reduces shrinkage.

Step 3 – Calculate F2

$$F2=API\left(0.02-3.57\times10^{-6}F1\right)+0.25$$

This step introduces the effect of crude oil quality through API gravity.

Purpose: Apply oil gravity correction to the shrinkage correlation.

Light oils (high API) normally contain more dissolved gas and therefore tend to shrink more.

Step 4 – Calculate Volume Parameter (Fvol)

$$F_{vol}=F1\sqrt{\frac{F2}{yg_{adj}}}+1.25T_{sep}$$

This step combines all major field variables:

  • Separator Pressure
  • Separator Temperature
  • Gas Gravity
  • Oil API Gravity
Purpose: Generate an empirical volume parameter that represents the fluid behavior.

This is one of the most important intermediate values in the calculation.

Step 5 – Calculate Oil Formation Volume Factor (Bo)

$$B_o=0.9759+12\times10^{-5}(F_{vol})^{1.2}$$

The Oil Formation Volume Factor (Bo) represents the relationship between oil volume at separator conditions and oil volume at stock tank conditions.

Purpose: Estimate the volume correction factor for the crude oil.

A larger Bo indicates a greater difference between separator and stock tank volumes.

Step 6 – Calculate Shrinkage Factor

$$Shrinkage=\frac{1}{B_o}$$

The final shrinkage factor is simply the inverse of the Oil Formation Volume Factor.

Purpose: Estimate how much separator oil remains as stock tank oil after gas liberation.
Interpretation

If Shrinkage Factor = 0.84

100 bbl Separator Oil ↓ 84 bbl Stock Tank Oil

This means 16% of the apparent separator volume was lost due to gas coming out of solution.

Complete Calculation Flow

Inputs ↓ Gas Gravity Correction (ygadj) ↓ F1 ↓ F2 ↓ Fvol ↓ Bo ↓ Shrinkage Factor

Temperature Volume Correction (60°F)

Crude oil expands when temperature increases and contracts when temperature decreases. For this reason, oil volumes are normally corrected to the industry standard reference temperature of 60°F.

V60 = Vobs / [ 1 + α × (Tobs − 60) ]

Where:

  • Vobs = Observed oil volume at measured temperature.
  • V60 = Corrected oil volume at 60°F.
  • Tobs = Observed oil temperature (°F).
  • α = Thermal expansion coefficient of crude oil.

Typical α Values

Crude Type α (1/°F)
Heavy Oil 0.00030
Medium Oil 0.00035
Light Oil 0.00040 – 0.00045

Example

Observed Volume = 1000 BBL
Temperature = 100°F
α = 0.00040

ΔT = 100 − 60 = 40°F
V60 = 1000 / (1 + 0.00040 × 40)
V60 = 984.25 BBL

Therefore, an observed volume of 1000 BBL at 100°F is equivalent to approximately 984.25 BBL at 60°F.

In Well Testing, temperature correction is usually applied before shrinkage correction:

Meter Oil Rate → Temperature Correction (CTL) → Oil @ 60°F → Shrinkage Correction → Stock Tank Oil

What Does Bo Mean?

The Oil Formation Volume Factor (Bo) indicates the relationship between the oil volume at separator conditions and the stabilized oil volume at stock tank conditions.

Bo > 1 means the oil occupies a larger volume before stabilization because it still contains dissolved gas.

What Does Shrinkage Factor Mean?

A Shrinkage Factor of 0.838 means that only 83.8% of the separator oil volume remains after the dissolved gas has escaped.

1.000 bbl Separator Oil ↓ 0.838 STB Stock Tank Oil

Volume Loss Calculation

Volume Loss = 1 - 0.838 = 0.162 = 16.2%

Therefore, approximately 16.2% of the apparent separator oil volume is lost due to gas liberation and stabilization.

Engineering Interpretation

If the separator measures 1,000 barrels of oil, the expected stock tank oil volume will be:

Stock Tank Oil = 1000 × 0.838 = 838 STB

This means approximately 162 barrels represent shrinkage caused by dissolved gas leaving the oil.

The Shrinkage Factor is extremely important during well testing because it allows engineers to estimate stock tank oil production when laboratory PVT data is not available.

Module 6 – Engineering Interpretation

The Shrinkage Factor indicates how much separator oil remains after the dissolved gas is released and the oil reaches stock tank conditions.

A higher shrinkage factor means less gas was dissolved in the oil. A lower shrinkage factor means more gas escaped from the oil.

Case 1 – Shrinkage Factor = 0.95

Shrinkage = 0.95

This means 95% of the separator oil volume remains as stock tank oil.

1000 bbl Separator Oil ↓ 950 STB Stock Tank Oil
Interpretation: Very little gas was liberated from the crude oil.
  • Low Gas-Oil Ratio (GOR)
  • Heavy or moderately heavy crude
  • Small volume reduction
  • Minimal shrinkage losses

Case 2 – Shrinkage Factor = 0.85

Shrinkage = 0.85

This means 85% of the separator oil volume remains after stabilization.

1000 bbl Separator Oil ↓ 850 STB Stock Tank Oil
Interpretation: Normal light crude oil behavior.
  • Moderate dissolved gas content
  • Typical well test conditions
  • Common shrinkage range for many oil wells
  • Approximately 15% volume loss

Case 3 – Shrinkage Factor = 0.75

Shrinkage = 0.75

This means only 75% of the separator oil volume remains after gas liberation.

1000 bbl Separator Oil ↓ 750 STB Stock Tank Oil
Interpretation: Large amount of gas was released from the crude oil.
  • High Gas-Oil Ratio (GOR)
  • Very light crude oil
  • Large volume reduction
  • Significant shrinkage losses

Module 7 – Factors Affecting Shrinkage

Shrinkage is not a fixed value. It changes depending on well conditions, separator operating conditions, and fluid properties.

Understanding these factors helps engineers predict stock tank oil volume more accurately during well testing operations.

1. Separator Pressure

Separator Pressure ↑ ↓ Shrinkage Factor ↑

Higher separator pressure keeps more gas dissolved inside the oil. Since less gas escapes, the oil volume decreases less.

Higher Separator Pressure = Less Gas Liberation = Less Shrinkage

Example:

  • Separator at 1000 psig → Less shrinkage
  • Separator at 200 psig → More shrinkage

2. Separator Pressure Decrease

Separator Pressure ↓ ↓ Shrinkage Factor ↓

When separator pressure decreases, more dissolved gas flashes out of solution. This causes a greater reduction in oil volume.

Lower Separator Pressure = More Gas Released = More Shrinkage

3. Gas Gravity (γg)

Gas Gravity ↑ ↓ Changes Dissolved Gas Behavior

Gas gravity indicates how heavy or light the gas is compared with air.

  • Low Gas Gravity → Lighter Gas
  • High Gas Gravity → Heavier Gas

Different gas compositions affect how gas dissolves in crude oil and how easily it separates.

Gas gravity affects shrinkage indirectly through gas solubility behavior.

4. Oil API Gravity

API Gravity ↑ ↓ Shrinkage Usually ↑

Light oils generally contain more dissolved gas than heavy oils.

  • 20 API → Heavy Oil
  • 45 API → Light Oil

When pressure drops, light oils release larger quantities of gas, resulting in greater shrinkage.

Higher API = More Dissolved Gas = Higher Shrinkage Potential

5. Separator Temperature

Temperature ↑ ↓ More Gas Liberation

Increasing temperature reduces gas solubility in crude oil.

As temperature rises, gas escapes more easily from the liquid phase.

Higher Temperature = More Gas Released = More Shrinkage

Example:

  • Separator at 70°F → Lower shrinkage
  • Separator at 120°F → Higher shrinkage

Engineering Summary

Factor Increase Effect on Shrinkage
Separator Pressure ↑ Less Shrinkage
Separator Temperature ↑ More Shrinkage
API Gravity ↑ Usually More Shrinkage
Gas Gravity ↑ Changes Gas Behavior
Easy Rule to Remember

High Pressure → Less Shrinkage

High Temperature → More Shrinkage

High API Oil → More Shrinkage

Gas Gravity affects how gas dissolves and separates from oil.

Module 8 – Field Applications of Shrinkage Factor

Shrinkage Factor is one of the most important calculations used in well testing and production engineering. It helps engineers estimate how much oil volume will remain after gas is separated from the crude oil.

Since separator oil volume is always larger than stock tank oil volume, the shrinkage factor is used to convert field measurements into realistic production figures.

1. Well Testing Operations

During well testing, oil production is usually measured at the separator. However, the client normally requires production rates in Stock Tank Barrels (STB).

Shrinkage Factor converts separator oil volume into stock tank oil volume.

Example:

  • Separator Oil = 1,000 BBL/day
  • Shrinkage = 0.85
  • Stock Tank Oil = 850 STB/day

2. Production Testing

During production tests, engineers need accurate estimates of stabilized oil production.

Shrinkage Factor helps determine the actual saleable oil volume after gas separation.

Essential for calculating realistic production rates.

3. Temporary Well Test Facilities

Temporary separators used during exploration or appraisal testing usually do not have complete laboratory PVT data available.

Engineers use empirical shrinkage correlations to estimate stock tank production directly in the field.

Provides quick production estimates without laboratory analysis.

4. Separator Performance Evaluation

Shrinkage calculations help evaluate how effectively the separator removes gas from the oil.

Changes in separator pressure and temperature can significantly affect shrinkage results.

Useful for optimizing separator operating conditions.

5. Estimating Stock Tank Oil Production

One of the primary uses of shrinkage factor is estimating Stock Tank Oil (STO) production.

Stock Tank Oil = Separator Oil × Shrinkage Factor

This allows production engineers to report actual recoverable oil volumes.

6. Production Reporting

Production reports submitted to operators and regulatory authorities are normally based on stock tank oil volumes rather than separator volumes.

Shrinkage Factor is therefore a critical reporting parameter.

Ensures accurate daily, monthly, and annual production reporting.

7. PVT Approximation When Laboratory Data Is Unavailable

Laboratory PVT studies provide the most accurate fluid properties, but they are not always available during early well testing operations.

Shrinkage correlations provide a practical alternative for estimating:

  • Oil Formation Volume Factor (Bo)
  • Stock Tank Oil Volume
  • Expected Shrinkage
  • Production Performance
Empirical shrinkage models are widely used until laboratory PVT data becomes available.

Shrinkage Factor bridges the gap between what is measured at the separator and the actual oil volume that reaches the stock tank.

Without shrinkage calculations, production rates can be significantly overestimated.

Module 9 – Limitations of the Shrinkage Calculator

Although the Shrinkage Factor Calculator is a useful engineering tool, it is important to understand its limitations before using the results for critical reservoir or production decisions.

1. Empirical Correlation Only

The calculator is based on an empirical correlation developed from field observations and historical production data.

It does not directly measure fluid properties. Instead, it estimates shrinkage using relationships between separator pressure, temperature, gas gravity, and oil API gravity.

The calculator provides an estimate, not an exact measurement.

2. Not a Replacement for Laboratory PVT Analysis

Laboratory PVT (Pressure-Volume-Temperature) studies remain the most accurate method for determining fluid properties such as:

  • Oil Formation Volume Factor (Bo)
  • Bubble Point Pressure
  • Solution Gas-Oil Ratio (Rs)
  • Shrinkage Factor
  • Fluid Phase Behavior

The calculator should never be considered a substitute for a complete laboratory PVT report.

For reservoir studies and reserves calculations, laboratory PVT data should always be used whenever available.

3. Accuracy Decreases for Unusual Fluids

The correlation was developed for typical crude oil systems. Accuracy may decrease when dealing with unusual reservoir fluids such as:

  • Very heavy oil
  • Very light condensate
  • Volatile oil systems
  • High CO₂ content fluids
  • High H₂S content fluids
  • Retrograde condensate reservoirs

In these cases, actual fluid behavior may differ significantly from the correlation prediction.

4. Best Used for Field Estimates and Quick Calculations

The primary purpose of the calculator is to provide rapid estimates during:

  • Well testing operations
  • Production testing
  • Temporary well test facilities
  • Early production evaluations
  • Daily field reporting
It is ideal when quick decisions are needed and laboratory data is not yet available.

5. Results Should Be Validated with Laboratory Data

Whenever laboratory PVT data becomes available, the estimated shrinkage factor should be compared against the laboratory results.

Any significant difference should be investigated and corrected in production calculations.

Field Estimate → Initial Decision

Laboratory PVT → Final Engineering Value

Key Takeaway

The Shrinkage Calculator is an excellent field engineering tool for estimating stock tank oil volumes, but it should be viewed as an approximation method rather than a substitute for laboratory PVT analysis.

Use it for quick operational decisions, and always verify the results when laboratory fluid data becomes available.

Quick Knowledge Check

Q1: What causes oil shrinkage?

Oil shrinkage occurs when pressure decreases and dissolved gas escapes from the crude oil. As gas leaves the liquid phase, the oil occupies less volume, causing shrinkage.

Q2: Why does gas come out of solution?

Gas remains dissolved in oil under high reservoir pressure. When pressure drops at the separator, the oil can no longer hold all the gas, causing the excess gas to come out of solution.

Q3: What is the purpose of Bo?

Bo (Oil Formation Volume Factor) represents the relationship between reservoir oil volume and stock tank oil volume. It is used to estimate how oil volume changes as pressure and gas content change.

Q4: What does API gravity represent?

API gravity measures how light or heavy an oil is compared to water. Higher API values indicate lighter oils, which generally contain more dissolved gas.

Q5: Why is gas gravity corrected?

Gas gravity is corrected to standard conditions because gas density changes with temperature. This correction improves the accuracy of shrinkage calculations.

Q6: What is separator pressure?

Separator pressure is the operating pressure inside the separator where oil and gas are separated. It strongly influences the amount of gas released from the oil.

Q7: What happens if separator pressure decreases?

Lower separator pressure causes more dissolved gas to flash out of the oil, resulting in greater shrinkage and lower stock tank oil volume.

Q8: What happens if separator temperature increases?

Higher separator temperature promotes gas liberation from the oil, which generally increases oil shrinkage.

Q9: Why is observed temperature required?

Observed temperature is used to correct the gas gravity value to standard conditions, ensuring more reliable shrinkage calculations.

Q10: What does F1 represent?

F1 is an intermediate pressure-related parameter that combines separator pressure and corrected gas gravity to estimate gas liberation effects.

Q11: What does F2 represent?

F2 is an oil gravity correction factor that adjusts the calculation based on the oil API gravity and separator conditions.

Q12: What does Fvol represent?

Fvol is an empirical volume parameter that combines pressure, temperature, gas gravity, and oil gravity effects into a single value used to estimate Bo.

Q13: How is Shrinkage calculated?

Shrinkage is calculated by taking the inverse of the Oil Formation Volume Factor:

Shrinkage = 1 / Bo

Q14: What does a shrinkage factor of 0.85 mean?

A shrinkage factor of 0.85 means that 1 barrel of separator oil will become approximately 0.85 stock tank barrels after gas separation.

Q15: What does a shrinkage factor of 0.95 mean?

A shrinkage factor of 0.95 indicates very little gas liberation. Most of the original oil volume remains after separation.

Q16: Where is shrinkage used in well testing?

Shrinkage is used to convert separator oil measurements into stock tank oil production, allowing engineers to estimate actual saleable oil volumes.

Q17: Why is laboratory PVT preferred?

Laboratory PVT analysis directly measures fluid properties under controlled conditions, making it more accurate than empirical shrinkage correlations.

Q18: Can shrinkage be greater than 1?

Normally no. Shrinkage factors are typically less than 1 because oil volume decreases when gas is removed. A value greater than 1 would indicate an error or unusual conditions.

Q19: What is Stock Tank Oil?

Stock Tank Oil (STO) is the stabilized oil remaining after gas has been separated and the oil has reached atmospheric conditions in storage tanks.

Q20: Why is shrinkage important for production reporting?

Production reports are based on stock tank oil volumes rather than separator volumes. Shrinkage ensures accurate reporting of the actual recoverable oil produced from the well.
