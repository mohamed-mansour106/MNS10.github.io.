Pressure Buildup Test Basics

Video lesson: Pressure Buildup Test Basics

Why Run a Build Up Test

A build up test shuts in a producing well and records how the pressure rises.

  • The well flows for some time, then it is shut in and the bottom hole pressure is recorded.
  • The rise of pressure tells about the reservoir pressure, the permeability and damage near the well (skin).
  • An advantage: the flow rate during shut-in is zero, which is easy to control.
  • A disadvantage: the well makes no production while it is shut in.
  • The analysis must also consider the flow period before the shut-in.
A build up test turns a shut-in well into a source of information about the reservoir.

How the Test Is Run

Good planning gives good data.

  • Flow the well at a steady rate long enough to settle, and record the rate.
  • Shut in the well, preferably as near to the zone as possible, so that wellbore storage is smaller.
  • Record pressure with a gauge of good range and accuracy, at a frequent rate at the start and a slower rate later.
  • Record the time of shut-in and keep the well shut for the planned time.
  • Avoid changes in the surface conditions during the test.
  • Follow your company and site procedures for shut-in and pressure work.
A steady flow before the test and a clean shut-in make a clean curve.

What the Pressure Curve Shows

The curve is read in three stages.

  • Early time: the wellbore storage effect. The pressure rises as the well fills and does not yet show the reservoir.
  • Middle time: the reservoir response. This part gives the permeability and the skin.
  • Late time: the effect of boundaries and the far field, and the approach to the average reservoir pressure.
  • Superposition means that the flow history before shut-in is added to the shut-in effect, so the analysis uses a special time function.
  • Engineers use special plots and software to read these stages.
Do not judge the reservoir from the early part of the curve. It is dominated by the well itself.

Common Problems and Good Practice

Many poor tests come from simple causes.

  • Too short a shut-in: the middle and late stages are never seen.
  • Unsteady flow before the shut-in makes the analysis harder.
  • Gauge drift or a wrong gauge range spoils the data.
  • Leaking valves let fluid pass during shut-in and the pressure behaves strangely.
  • Phase changes in the well, such as gas coming out or liquid falling back, change the pressure.
  • Keep a log of events with times so the engineer can explain every change.
The test log is as valuable as the gauge data. Write down every event with its time.
