Well Test Burners

Video lesson: Well Test Burners

Why We Burn and How the Flame Is Made

During a test the oil and gas cannot always be stored or sold. Burning them in a controlled way is the safe option. A well test burner is built to burn them as cleanly as possible.

  • Liquid burns well only when it is turned into very fine droplets. This is called atomizing.
  • The burner mixes the fine droplets with air so they burn completely.
  • Better mixing means less black smoke and less fallout on the ground.
  • A pilot flame keeps the main flame lit.
  • Some burners use water spray to cool the flame and to cut heat radiation.
A clean flame is a good sign. Black smoke or drops falling from the flame is a sign of poor atomizing.

What the Burner Needs

A burner is connected to several supplies. Each one has a job, and a failure in one of them affects the flame.

  • Well fluid: the oil and gas to burn, from the separator or the manifold.
  • Compressed air: from a dedicated air compressor. It atomizes the oil and supplies the oxygen. Never use the rig air supply for this.
  • Pilot fuel: usually propane, for the pilot flame.
  • Ignition: a safe, controlled way to light the pilot.
  • Water: for the water spray that cools the flame and protects the boom.
  • Check valves on the air and oil lines so the fluids cannot flow back.
The air and oil pressures must be in balance. If the oil pressure is too high compared with the air, the burner will smoke or drip.

Burner Types and Heads

Burners come in several sizes. The choice depends on the expected flow, the fluid and the rules of the site.

  • Single-head and multi-head burners: more heads can burn more fluid. Heads can be opened or closed to match the flow.
  • Clean-burning burners use good atomizing and a lot of air. They are used where smoke and fallout must be kept to a minimum.
  • Burners for mud and oil mixtures are built to handle a rough, dirty flow.
  • Gas flare: a simple stack for gas only. It burns gas, not oil.
  • A burner is placed on a boom, away from the rig, to keep the heat away and to burn in the wind direction.
Always pick the burner for the flow you expect. Using one head too many on a low flow gives a poor flame, and too few on a high flow gives smoke and fallout.

Running the Burner Safely

Burners are a source of fire and heat. Always follow your company procedure and site rules.

Before lighting

  • Check the wind direction. The flame should burn away from the rig and the people.
  • Check the air supply, pilot fuel, water and all valves.
  • Make sure the area is clear and everyone knows the plan.

Lighting and running

  • Light the pilot first, then bring the well fluid slowly.
  • Watch the flame: color, smoke and drips. Adjust the air, the oil and the number of heads.
  • Keep a watch on wind changes and be ready to adjust.

Stopping

  • Stop the oil first. Keep the air on until the flame is out.
  • Close the supplies in the right order, and leave the area safe.
If the flame goes out while fluid is still flowing, stop the flow and follow the procedure before you try to relight.
